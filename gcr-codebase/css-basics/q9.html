Inside Today's Edition: The Quiet Rise of Passkeys

Passwords have been the default for so long that it's easy to forget they were never a great idea to begin with. They're reused, they're phished, and they're forgotten constantly enough that "forgot password" flows are their own category of user experience design.

Passkeys quietly fix most of that. Built on public-key cryptography, they can't be phished the way a typed password can, because there's no shared secret for an attacker to trick out of you in the first place. Your device holds the private key; the service only ever sees a signed challenge.

Adoption has been gradual rather than dramatic. Big platforms rolled out support first, followed slowly by banks, and now smaller sites are starting to offer it as an option alongside traditional logins rather than a replacement for them.

The interesting part isn't the cryptography — it's the interface problem. Getting people to trust a login method that doesn't involve typing anything at all turns out to be a much harder sell than the security argument alone.

Even so, the direction is clear. Every year, fewer accounts are protected by something you know, and more by something you have — and that shift alone quietly closes off entire categories of attack.
